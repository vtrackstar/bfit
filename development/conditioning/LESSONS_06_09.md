# Conditioning Training — educational draft, chapters 6–9
2026-11-26 | Development branch only. Reader-facing copy and implementation specifications. Programming Audit V2 governs prescriptions; Engine V2/workflow are prototypes, not complete UI. Examples are illustrative, not actual readers or promises.

## Chapter 6 — Build your conditioning plan
### Where should I start?
I start with what you can comfortably do now, not what you hope to do in a month. Someone who wants to play a whole basketball game may need the same starting workout as someone who wants to walk farther. Their goals are different; their first step can be similar.

Choose your goal:
- **Build Stamina:** I want to keep going longer without tiring as quickly.
- **Improve Work Capacity:** I want to handle repeated challenging efforts and recover between them.
You can change your goal later. Neither goal requires all-out exercise.

### Choose your activity
Pick something you can safely access and enjoy repeating. Walking, marching at home, cycling, rowing, elliptical and stair machines are options. Running is available when you already run comfortably; if you are just learning to run, [Your First 5K](https://vtrackstar.github.io/bfit/milestones/first-5k/) or [Run Strong](https://vtrackstar.github.io/bfit/guides/run-strong/) may be a better starting point. Choose a familiar movement circuit if that is your preference. Swimming has its own easy practice and logging options; use a lifeguarded pool, swim with appropriate supervision and never alone. If you cannot safely complete a familiar pool length and rest at the wall, choose supervised lessons or a land-based activity instead. No breath-holding or underwater challenges.

### Tell me what you already do
The questions are about your current activity, not a test you have to pass. How many minutes can you comfortably sustain in this particular activity? If you are not sure, say so. If you can only walk for a minute or two, that's a useful starting point. Recent comfortable workouts, including their effort and how you felt the next day, can help establish a more suitable starting level. Lifting experience or cycling fitness does not automatically establish your readiness for running or another activity. Do not perform a maximum-effort test just to answer these questions.

We also need to know how many days and how much total time you have, what other exercise you already do, and whether more demanding exercise is appropriate. If you have symptoms, known relevant health conditions, restrictions or a recent illness/injury, follow the safety guidance and appropriate professional advice. The guide cannot clear a medical condition. Uncertainty about vigorous exercise is a reason not to escalate.

### Choose how the plan fits your week
**Make this my conditioning plan:** use the guide as your main source of conditioning. Most readers will choose two primary sessions, with an optional easy third session if recovered.
**Fit it around my current training:** tell me about your existing cardio, sport, classes, strength and demanding days. We'll account for what you already do rather than automatically adding exercise. There is no automatic sharing of workout history between BFit guides.
Only have one day? An easy or steady session is a useful start. If you are prepared and already get suitable aerobic work elsewhere, a controlled interval session may fit. One session is not equivalent to the full two-session plan.

### What level should I choose?
You do not have to choose a level yourself. The guide uses your recent activity and recovery to suggest a starting point.
| Starting point | What we practice |
|---|---|
| Establish | Short, manageable activity; use easy breaks if needed |
| Build | Longer steady activity, or introductory controlled intervals when ready |
| Develop | Sustained steady work or longer controlled efforts |
| Specialize | A suitable emphasis: longer endurance, tempo, aerobic power, repeatable efforts or familiar circuits |
These are coaching categories, not a ranking of how fit you are. You can stay at a level that suits your needs. Moderate circuits may be suitable earlier after learning the movements. More demanding intervals require their own preparation and your choice to try them.

### Example: your first visit
Fictional reader: can comfortably cycle for five minutes, has two days and wants to repeat demanding efforts eventually.
Suggested start: 5 minutes easy warm-up, 5 minutes easy cycling, 5 minutes easy cooldown (15 minutes total). Keep resistance light, adjust to comfortable conversation, stop or shorten as necessary. If five minutes continuous is too much, begin with shorter easy bouts and recover as needed. Your goal remains Improve Work Capacity while you establish a base. Later sessions can build the main activity gradually before introducing intervals. No requirement to reach a harder level by a certain date.

**Before starting:** check your available time, choose a familiar safe setup and read your first action. Shorten the session or reschedule if it does not fit. Keep your preparation and recovery time; there is no need to rush them.

## Chapter 7 — Your workouts
### What should I do when I open today's workout?
The first screen should tell you the activity, effort, total planned time and first action. Open details if you want to know why it is prescribed. You can begin without reading every lesson.
1. Set up your equipment safely and find a comfortable pace.
2. Warm up gradually at an easy effort.
3. Follow the main activity or work and recovery periods. Use the effort target, not someone else's speed.
4. Ease down during the cooldown when appropriate.
5. Record what you actually completed and how it felt.
If you feel unusually unwell, experience warning symptoms or new/worsening pain, stop and follow safety guidance. Do not finish a timer at the expense of your health.

### Your first steady session (illustrative)
| Part | Planned time | What to do |
|---|---:|---|
| Warm-up | 5 min | Start walking slowly or cycle with light resistance |
| Main activity | 15 min | Steady effort, 4–5/10; breathing faster but full sentences possible |
| Cooldown | 5 min | Gradually ease pace |
Total planned time: 25 minutes. This example is for someone who already tolerates 15 minutes comfortably; a less active reader may receive a shorter session. The main activity is not the total session time.

### Your first controlled interval session (illustrative)
For a prepared reader using a stationary bike:
| Part | Planned time | What to do |
|---|---:|---|
| Warm-up | 5 min | Pedal easily; build gradually |
| Work | 30 sec | Increase effort to 5–6/10, controlled |
| Recovery | 90 sec | Pedal easily or rest safely |
| Repeat work and recovery | 2 more rounds | Three work/recovery pairs total |
| Cooldown | 5 min | Ease the pace |
Main interval block: 6 minutes (90 seconds work, 270 seconds recovery). Total planned session: 16 minutes. Recovery after the last interval is included. Harder intervals require longer preparation; the guide does not ask you to sprint all-out.
If your first effort feels very hard, reduce your setting. If breathing does not settle during recovery, extend easy recovery once as appropriate. If still not ready, finish the demanding portion and cool down when safe. Warning symptoms override all timer instructions. Do not chase a heart-rate target.

### If you need a shorter visit
Say the planned workout is four one-minute challenging intervals with two minutes easy after each: 22 minutes including five minutes each of warm-up and cooldown. You have 20 minutes. Three pairs fit into 19 minutes. That's a useful shorter visit, not proof that you couldn't complete the original. Record it as a time-adjusted version. We can revisit the original prescription when time allows and recovery is appropriate. Do not remove the recovery just to fit another interval. If even the shorter version does not fit, choose an easy alternative or reschedule.

### What should I record?
Record the work you actually did, how hard it felt, whether the movements stayed controlled, whether you needed extra recovery, and why you ended early if applicable. If you know output (for example, cadence, distance or watts on the same machine), you can add it. You can leave unknown measurements blank. A timer tracks elapsed phases, not whether you physically exercised; your record still relies on your honest confirmation. Manual entries are labeled self-reported. Do not invent time from a plan or a pool distance. Swimming is recorded after leaving the water, with actual lengths, pool size and units, time if known and wall rest if known.
The following day, check whether recovery feels normal for you, you feel more tired than usual, or fatigue is limiting your usual activities. If you have pain or concerning symptoms, use the separate safety route. Do not mark normal recovery on the same day merely to progress.

### When life interrupts the workout
If you need a break, pause. If you have to leave, finish early and record what actually happened. If you did not start, skip or reschedule. An easy visit or rest day may be the right choice. The guide should never add a second demanding session just to catch up. A missed session is not an exercise failure.
Timer limitations: a phone browser may suspend timers when the screen locks or the app is backgrounded. Confirm the actual phases before saving; no automatic exercise credit for uncertain time. Do not operate a phone while swimming, moving on stairs or when it would be unsafe. Use the manual option if the timer is impractical.

## Chapter 8 — Combine conditioning with your other training
### Do I need to add more cardio?
Maybe not. If you already play basketball, attend classes or follow a running plan, you're doing physical work that contributes to your week. I want to see what you're already doing before adding another demanding workout. Existing activity can satisfy part of the plan's workload without being treated as a measured interval test.
The priority is what you most want to develop and what you can recover from. Do not count the same activity twice. Only recorded, qualifying aerobic minutes count toward general aerobic activity targets; the whole length of a game, strength workout or class is not automatically aerobic work.

### Make the week fit your goal
| Your current routine | How I would approach conditioning |
|---|---|
| Foundation Training or other strength plan | Keep your lifting schedule; start with manageable conditioning around it. |
| Athleticism | Protect sprinting, jumping and other high-quality practice. Avoid doing demanding conditioning immediately before those sessions. |
| Run Strong or Your First 5K | Count relevant running sessions; add another mode only if useful, rather than automatically adding intervals. |
| Weekend Warrior | Use conditioning in place of suitable cardio slots; keep the short weekday time budget. |
| Build Your Glutes | Keep the intended strength work and avoid adding lower-body fatigue that prevents quality leg sessions. |
| Group fitness or recreational sport | Account for demanding classes and games; an easy session or rest may be more useful than extra HIIT. |
If strength or power is the priority and activities must be combined, perform the priority work while fresh; separate demanding sessions when feasible. Easy exercise can sometimes fit on the same day. This is a practical starting approach, not a rule that cardio and lifting cannot coexist. [Concurrent aerobic and resistance training systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34757594/). Individual preferences, schedule, workload and recovery matter. Keep competitive sport-specific planning outside the automated scope.

### Three example weeks
These examples teach integration, not prescriptions for every reader. Assume the activities are familiar and suitable.
| Day | New exerciser | Strength-focused adult | Recreational basketball player |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Easy walking | Upper-body lifting + easy cycling if recovered | Easy conditioning |
| Tue | Rest or normal daily movement | Lower-body lifting | Rest or other easy activity |
| Wed | Rest or normal daily movement | Rest | Strength |
| Thu | Easy walking | Upper-body lifting + easy cycling if recovered | Easy activity or rest |
| Fri | Rest or normal daily movement | Lower-body lifting | Rest |
| Sat | Optional easy walk | Rest | Basketball game (existing demanding work) |
| Sun | Rest | Rest or easy activity | Rest |
The basketball example does not add a dedicated interval session by default. It is not intended as a complete basketball performance program; a game is not an exact substitute for a controlled aerobic-power prescription. If two existing demanding sessions already make you unusually fatigued, do not add another. You can rearrange days to suit your life. A new interval user should generally leave room for an easy/rest day between demanding lower-body sessions where practical. If a hard slot doesn't fit, use easy work or leave it out.

### What if my schedule changes?
Tell the guide about your new availability and existing training. Keep prior logs; change future plans rather than rewriting what happened. Back-to-back easy visits can be appropriate if recovered. A hard visit requires preparation and appropriate recovery. Do not double a missed hard session, shorten all rests to fit a busy day, or assume an optional session is owed.
If you have been away for a week or more, repeat an appropriate previous session or choose an easier visit. After about two weeks away from that activity, use a reduced return workout, without hard efforts initially, and reassess recovery. These time windows are guide defaults, not precise detraining thresholds. After illness or injury, seek appropriate individual guidance. Do not use the schedule to override safety concerns.

**Take this into your week:** Choose the sessions that serve your goal. Leave space for your other training and recovery. If a new conditioning workout makes the activity you care about consistently worse, review the plan instead of automatically adding more work.

## Chapter 9 — How do I know if I'm improving?
### What does progress actually look like?
A workout can become easier before you see a big change in speed or distance. You might sustain an activity longer, maintain a similar output across intervals, or feel less challenged at the same setting. These are useful observations under comparable conditions. No single workout proves that your conditioning improved or declined.
| Goal | Useful observations |
|---|---|
| Build Stamina | Longer comfortable duration; similar effort at a familiar pace; lower effort at comparable workload |
| Improve Work Capacity | Similar output across efforts; more completed controlled work; less need for extra recovery |
| Mixed-movement conditioning | Controlled movement through the intended work periods; repeatability; how the muscles and breathing respond |
| Swimming | More familiar lengths at manageable effort; time if known; appropriate wall recovery |
Do not compare different machines' output numbers as if they are interchangeable. Record the activity, machine/settings, units and any important changes. For example, 80 rpm at the same bike resistance can be a useful comparison, but 80 rpm at different resistances or on a different bike does not necessarily represent the same workload. Weather, terrain, rest, effort and technique matter.

### Two fictional progress examples
Example A: steady cycling, same bike and resistance, comparable effort.
| Visit | Main activity | Effort |
|---|---:|---:|
| Earlier | 15 min | 4/10 |
| Recent | 18 min | 4/10 |
This reader sustained the activity for longer at a similar reported effort. That is encouraging; it is not a measured increase in VO2 max. Confirm the pattern over more visits.

Example B: four one-minute efforts with two minutes recovery, same bike and resistance.
| Effort | Earlier rpm | Recent rpm |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1 | 80 | 80 |
| 2 | 77 | 79 |
| 3 | 73 | 78 |
| 4 | 70 | 78 |
Earlier output fell 12.5% from first to last; recent fell 2.5%. Both values are descriptive, not a clinical threshold. If effort and recovery were comparable, the recent visit suggests more consistent execution. Starting more conservatively, familiarization and measurement variation can also affect the result; no claim of improved aerobic power from this alone. Visual plan: labeled line chart plus table and textual takeaway; no chart when data are not comparable.

### Why didn't my workout get harder?
I want you to be able to repeat a workload before making it more demanding. Our default is two comparable successful sessions at the current prescription with controlled work, appropriate effort and normal next-day recovery before considering a small progression. If the prescription changes, we begin the comparison again. That means the next week may look exactly like the last one. Repeating is a useful part of training.
| What happened | What we consider next |
|---|---|
| Two comparable, controlled visits; normal recovery | A small progression if suitable |
| Only one successful visit, or recovery not yet recorded | Repeat or hold until we have the needed information |
| Stopped early because time was short | Consider original plan when time and recovery permit |
| Effort was excessive or technique deteriorated | Reduce work, adjust activity or rest |
| Switched activities | Establish a new baseline |
| Concerning symptoms or new/worsening pain | Stop the provoking activity; follow safety guidance |
Progress may be a few extra steady minutes or one additional interval; higher intensities need independent readiness and opt-in. A shorter rest is not the default progression. The two-visit rule is a conservative coaching choice, not a universal physiological threshold.

### Your four-week review
Every four weeks, look at what you actually did rather than assuming the calendar changed your fitness. See full, shortened and skipped guide visits separately, actual known main work and recovery by activity, outside activities separately, and missing recovery information. Prior training history helps choose a starting point but is not counted as guide attendance. If you only know swimming lengths, show lengths and mark time unknown. Never total planned exercise as actual completed work. Optional attendance should not penalize the reader.
Fictional review: two main visits per week scheduled, seven full visits and one shortened for time, 132 minutes of known main work, three outside activities recorded separately, no measured VO2 max. At the same bike resistance, two 18-minute visits felt manageable and next-day recovery was normal. Suggested next action: consider adding a small amount to one comparable steady session, subject to the next-session readiness and schedule check. If the reader did not log the comparable effort/recovery evidence, say what is missing; do not generate the same conclusion.
If you are comfortable with your routine and it serves your goal, keep it. If the goal changes, select a different emphasis. If the program is not working around your week, change the schedule before assuming you need to exercise harder.

### Keep your records
Your records are intended to stay in the browser on the device you use. Clearing browser data or using a different device can remove access. Use the backup and restore option when available, and keep a copy of your records if you want them later. Do not assume BFit accounts, cloud syncing or shared records between guides. Downloading a record does not automatically prove exercise occurred. The guide does not require bodyweight, photos, calorie-burn estimates or heart-rate data to show useful progress.
**Take this into your next session:** Pick a clear measurement you can repeat, record actual work and effort, check recovery the following day, and let the next recommendation reflect your goal and your recent sessions.
