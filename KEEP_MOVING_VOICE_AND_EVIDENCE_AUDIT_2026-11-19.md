## The writing standard

Start by saying **what something is, what it does, or what the reader should do**. Put context immediately afterward when it helps someone understand the guidance.

For example:

- **Visual:** “BFit illustration of how muscle fibers may adapt to resistance training.”
- **Research:** “The LIFTMOR study followed screened postmenopausal women in a supervised program. Ask your clinician about appropriate exercise if you have low bone density.”
- **Workout:** “Choose a sturdy, nonrolling chair.”
- **Saving:** “Your choices stay in this browser. Download a backup to keep another copy.”

Use an explicit restriction when it is part of safe instructions. Recent falls, unexplained dizziness, pain, and medically restricted activity warrant clear directions. Evidence limitations also belong alongside research claims when the study population or conditions matter.

## Standard for future BFit edits

Write the useful sentence first. Remove extra disclaimers, rhetorical contrasts, stacked slogans, generic motivational copy, and repeated qualifications. Keep the explanations readers need to make a sound training decision.

Check the words against the actual feature, the exercise shown, and the cited study. Clear authorship and source credits matter more than attempts to imitate a particular kind of narration.
