Your First 5K

Build toward five kilometers at a pace that fits your current fitness. Walking breaks are welcome.

Five kilometers is about 3.1 miles. You can finish with walking breaks or work toward running the whole distance. Most of your training will be comfortable running and walking. Some runners may need several months or longer. Your progress depends on your starting point, schedule and recovery.

I designed this program to help you build toward your first finish, one workout at a time.

    Make running feel easier
    How fast should I run?
    Use the talk test. During most workouts you should be able to speak a full sentence without stopping to catch your breath. Slow down or walk if you cannot. Your pace is the time it takes to cover a mile or kilometer. A smaller number means a faster pace. The timer tells you how long to run, not how fast.
    Walking breaks give you time to catch your breath and let you accumulate more practice. They count toward a run-and-walk 5K.
    How should running feel?
    Keep your shoulders relaxed, look ahead and let your arms swing naturally. Use comfortable steps rather than reaching far ahead with your feet. You do not need a particular foot strike or step count. Start on a flat, familiar route. Walk for five minutes before running and three minutes afterward when your workout includes running.
    When should I slow down or stop?
    Breathing harder and feeling your muscles work can be normal. Sharp or localized pain, pain that changes how you move, dizziness or unusual shortness of breath are reasons to stop. If you experience chest pain, fainting or severe breathing difficulty during exercise, stop and seek emergency medical help. Seek medical guidance before resuming after concerning symptoms.
    Why does my workout change?
    After you log a workout and confirm recovery the next day, the plan uses your recent training to decide whether to build, repeat or reduce the next session. Repeating a workout is part of training. Every four weeks, look at your completed sessions, running minutes and longest comfortable run. There is no deadline to reach the next phase.
    Using your watch or treadmill
    Record your running and walking intervals separately from the warm-up and cooldown if possible. Elapsed time includes all breaks; moving time may exclude stops. For your 5K result, use the measured distance and elapsed time of the main outing. You can enter miles, kilometers or meters. The timer does not measure distance or sync with your watch.
    On a treadmill, use a comfortable speed and the safety clip. Start flat. On hills, keep the same comfortable effort rather than trying to maintain your flat-route pace.
    

    Fit training into your week
    Choose your running days
    Two-day example: Tuesday and Saturday. Three-day example: Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday. Keep a day between running workouts. Your longer outing builds time on your feet; practice days help you get familiar with running. If you already play sports or train hard, tell the plan about strenuous activity before your next run.
    Strength training alongside running
    Strength training is optional. It can support running and everyday movement. If you are new to it, try one or two easy sessions each week: chair squats, calf raises, supported step-ups and a hip hinge. Start with one or two sets of 8 to 12 controlled repetitions. Keep enough energy for your running. If you already lift, use your existing routine rather than adding another full program. Avoid hard leg training immediately before your longer outing.
    Learn the basics in Foundation Training.
    Walking, biking and swimming on other days
    Easy walking, biking or swimming can help you stay active between runs. Keep the effort comfortable enough to talk, and take a full rest day if you need one. These activities can support endurance without adding more running impact, but they do not count as completed running workouts. Tell the plan about hard exercise before your next run. If running hurts, stop and seek appropriate advice rather than trying to train through pain with another activity.
    Missed a workout or feeling tired?
    Continue on your next available day rather than squeezing missed runs together. After a longer break, the plan may reduce the workout or ask you to reassess. If you feel unusually tired, rest and check in again when you recover. Regular sleep, meals and water help support training.
    Shoes, weather and routes
    Choose comfortable shoes that fit securely. Expensive racing shoes are optional. Use well-lit routes and pay attention to traffic. In heat or humidity, slow down, choose cooler hours and take walking breaks. If conditions are unsafe, postpone the session or use an indoor option.
    

    Prepare for your first 5K
    Choose a measured route, track or treadmill. Your longer workouts will gradually prepare you for the distance. A 400-meter track takes 12.5 laps to cover 5 km in lane one, starting from the appropriate mark. Use your watch or a measured route to confirm distance.
    On the day, start at a familiar comfortable effort. Use the run-and-walk rhythm you practiced if that is your goal. Stop at five kilometers or the prescribed time limit, whichever comes first. If you reach the time limit first, record what you did and continue training. After you finish, walk to cool down and give yourself time to recover.
    Your result can help you choose a new goal. A 35-minute 5K averages 7 minutes per kilometer, or about 11 minutes 16 seconds per mile. That average describes your outing; it does not automatically become your training pace.
