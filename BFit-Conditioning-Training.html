Chapter 1

What is conditioning?

Why do I get tired so quickly?

Carrying groceries upstairs takes strength. Making several trips without needing a long break also takes endurance. When you play basketball, getting down the court once is different from doing it throughout a game.

Conditioning helps you sustain physical activity, handle repeated efforts and manage fatigue. I use work capacity in this guide to mean how much physical work you can handle and repeat; there is no single universal work-capacity score.

What you want to improveWhat it means
Cardiorespiratory fitnessHow effectively your body delivers and uses oxygen during activity
Aerobic enduranceHow long you can sustain activity that relies heavily on oxygen
Aerobic powerHow rapidly your aerobic system can supply energy; maximal oxygen uptake (VO₂ max) measures maximal oxygen uptake, not mechanical power
Muscular enduranceHow long a muscle can keep working
Repeated-effort abilityHow well you can repeat demanding efforts
Movement economyHow much energy a familiar activity takes at a given workload

These abilities overlap. Your legs may tire on stairs before breathing becomes demanding. That can give you a clue about what to work on, but it cannot diagnose what is limiting you. A cyclist may have good aerobic fitness and still need practice with running’s movement and impact. Research suggests some fitness transfers between these activities, but it does not establish that they are interchangeable. Running/cycling transfer review (specific to those activities).

What changes with training?

Your heart pumps blood to your muscles. Tiny blood vessels help deliver oxygen, and structures inside muscle cells called mitochondria use oxygen to help supply energy. Regular endurance training can improve aspects of oxygen delivery and use, making everyday movement easier for some people. Improvements depend on your starting fitness and training. Cardiovascular adaptations review; Muscle mitochondria and capillary adaptations review.

Choose what matters to you

Build Stamina — keep going longer. Start at a manageable pace and build duration.

Improve Work Capacity — recover between efforts. Develop a base, then practice controlled intervals when ready. Both goals can start with easy activity.

Explore the science: How much activity is useful?

WHO recommends adults work toward 150–300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity weekly or 75–150 minutes vigorous activity, or an equivalent combination, and strengthening all major muscle groups on at least two days. Activity supports health, and some is better than none. These are long-term public-health targets, not starting requirements. Our plan may initially provide less. Count actual qualifying activity, not all workout, circuit or timer minutes. BFit effort ratings are not exact public-health intensity categories. WHO activity guidance; CDC adult activity overview.

Chapter 2

How your body produces energy

Why does the start of a workout sometimes feel harder?

Ever start cycling or walking, then find that your breathing feels more comfortable a few minutes later? Some people call that a second wind.

Your muscles need more energy as soon as you start moving. Oxygen uptake increases gradually after you start; the exact response depends on the effort and your individual physiology. At a sustainable constant pace, aerobic energy supply can rise toward the new demand. Warming up and finding a comfortable rhythm may also help you feel better. This is one possible explanation, not proof of what causes every second wind. You may never notice one. Starting too fast can make the effort keep getting harder. Poole and Jones, oxygen uptake kinetics.

Try a gradual warm-up

Walk slowly before walking briskly; on a bike, start with light resistance. Increase effort gradually until you reach the planned pace. The American Heart Association suggests five to ten minutes for a warm-up. For very short, easy introductory visits, I use shorter gradual transitions as a coaching modification, not a universal warm-up recommendation. Ease down at the end when appropriate. If the effort is too hard, slow down. Stop for warning symptoms rather than waiting for relief. AHA warm-up and cool-down.

Explore the science: ATP and energy systems

ATP is a molecule your muscles use to contract. Your body continually rebuilds it using overlapping pathways.

PathwayWhat it doesExample
PhosphagenUses stored phosphocreatine to rapidly restore ATPBrief jump
GlycolyticBreaks down glucose or stored carbohydrate to help make ATP quicklyDemanding sustained burst
Oxidative (aerobic)Uses oxygen to help make ATP from carbohydrates and fatsSustained activity and recovery

Anaerobic means the pathway does not directly require oxygen, not that your body contains no oxygen. All three contribute together; their relative contributions change with demand. No fixed crossover time or exact percentages apply to every activity. Gastin, 2001; Baker et al., 2010.

Explore the science: The oxygen deficit

During the adjustment to a sustainable constant workload, the gap between total energy demand and oxidative energy supply is supported by nonoxidative pathways. This is related to the oxygen deficit. At harder intensities oxygen uptake may continue rising rather than settle. This is not a graph of how hard exercise feels.

A clinical second-wind phenomenon in McArdle disease, a rare muscle metabolic disorder, is distinct from the everyday sensation described above. Severe exercise-related muscle symptoms or dark urine warrant urgent medical attention; do not self-diagnose or push through. GeneReviews.

Chapter 3

Understanding fatigue and recovery

Why can't I keep the pace I started with?

If you pedal as fast as possible during your first interval, you may struggle to repeat the effort later. Feeling tired and a measurable reduction in what you can do are related but different. Many processes affect performance, including muscle energy supply, nervous-system function and perceived effort. Girard et al., 2011 (repeated-sprint fatigue review).

What you noticeWhat to try
Breathing heavier than plannedSlow down; recover comfortably
Legs or arms tire firstReduce resistance or choose an easier familiar movement
Pace fades across effortsStart more conservatively, use recovery, reduce work if needed
Technique becomes difficult to controlEnd that effort; rest or simplify
New pain or concerning symptomsStop and follow the safety guidance

These observations are not diagnoses. Normal exertion is expected; unexpected or severe symptoms need appropriate attention.

Why do my muscles burn?

The burning sensation involves chemical changes in working muscle and signals from sensory nerves. It has no single universal cause. Lactate is produced during metabolism and can be used as fuel. It is neither simply waste nor a complete explanation of fatigue. Next-day soreness is a different experience; neither soreness nor burning is needed to prove a workout worked. Lactate and delayed soreness review. Brooks, lactate shuttle theory.

What is recovery for?

Recovery helps you prepare for the next effort. You may move slowly (active recovery) or rest (passive recovery), depending on the activity and goal. Neither is universally better. On land, slow down until your breathing is comfortable; extend easy recovery once if appropriate. If you still do not feel ready, finish the demanding portion and cool down when safe. Swimming recovery is at the wall. Stop immediately for warning symptoms.

Explore the science: Energy restoration

Aerobic ATP production helps restore phosphocreatine between efforts. Feeling ready or seeing a lower heart rate does not prove full energy restoration. Aerobic fitness can support repeated-effort recovery, alongside muscle capacity, effort intensity and rest. Bishop et al., repeated-sprint training review (sport-specific evidence; controlled guide intervals are not maximal sprints).

Chapter 4

How hard should I work?

Why can the same workout feel different?

You might use the same bike resistance and speed on two days but feel more tired on the second. The setting is the external workload (what you do); your breathing, heart rate and effort are the internal response (how your body responds). If the same setting feels harder today, slow down or lower the resistance.

Start at a comfortable pace

Start slowly in a familiar activity. When it feels comfortable, try a full sentence if talking is safe. Gradually adjust one setting until you reach the effort in your plan. Do not test your maximum. Stop for concerning symptoms.

BFit effort rating (0–10)How it may feel
Easy 2–3Comfortable breathing and conversation
Steady 4–5Breathing faster; full sentences possible
Challenging 6–7Short phrases; demanding but controlled
Hard 8Few words comfortably; hard but repeatable
9–10Near-maximal/maximal; not prescribed here

Zero is rest, ten is maximal effort. RPE means rating of perceived exertion. These are approximate coaching cues, not laboratory thresholds, Run Strong's five numbered zones or the CDC's numeric moderate/vigorous classifications. The CDC talk test is another practical way to estimate effort. CDC intensity guidance; ACSM/ESSA intensity consensus.

Set up your activity and learn the movements

Practice at an easy pace before using a timer. Choose a familiar activity you can perform safely. If you use a machine for the first time, ask gym staff to show you the controls and how to stop it.

Walking or marching

Choose an even route or clear floor space. Stand comfortably tall, take natural steps, and keep your shoulders relaxed. To adjust the effort, change your pace rather than forcing a longer stride.

Stationary bike

Adjust the seat before mounting so your knee stays slightly bent at the bottom of the pedal stroke. Start at low resistance and pedal smoothly. If unfamiliar with the bike, ask staff to help set it up.

Rowing machine

Set the foot straps and manageable resistance. Push with your legs, then open your hips slightly and pull the handle toward your lower ribs. Return the arms, torso, then knees. Practice before timed work.

Elliptical or stair machine

Use the handles for balance, set a low speed or resistance, and keep your steps smooth. Learn the stop control before starting. If balance or the movement is difficult, choose walking or cycling instead.

Running or swimming

Use an activity you already tolerate. For running-specific practice, see Run Strong. Swim a familiar stroke in a lifeguarded pool with a buddy, rest at the wall, and log afterward.

Four familiar circuit stations

These are practice instructions, not an extra workout. Use the dose in your plan. Move at a controlled pace, and rest or simplify a movement if you cannot maintain control.

1. Chair stand (chair squat)

Place a sturdy chair against a wall. Sit near the front with feet flat. Lean slightly forward, stand up, then sit back down slowly. Use armrests if needed.

Chair stand in Build Your Glutes · Illustrated sit-to-stand instructions (NHS)

2. Standing march

Stand on a clear, level surface. Lift one foot a comfortable height, return it to the floor, then alternate. Keep your steps smooth; use stable support for balance if needed.

Marching illustration and warm-up video (NHS). Use your plan’s duration, not the full warm-up routine.

3. Incline push-up

Place both hands on a stable raised surface that cannot slide or tip. Step back, keep your body straight, bend your elbows to bring your chest toward the surface, and push away. A wall or higher surface is an easier option.

Learn the movement in Your First Push-Up · Countertop push-up video (@clairedefitt, YouTube) · Wall push-up illustration (NHS)

4. Supported low step-up

Use a stable low step and secure support for balance. Put your whole foot on the step, stand up, then step down under control. Alternate the leading leg. If the setup is unavailable or balance is difficult, march instead.

More technique in Foundation Training · Step-ups with light support (Physitrack)

Explore the science: Why does my watch seem late?

Heart rate takes time to respond when workload changes; it can remain elevated during recovery. Do not speed up simply because your watch hasn't reached a number in a short interval. Use effort, repeatability and a familiar workload. HR and oxygen uptake during interval recovery, retrospective study (does not establish every response curve).

Explore the science: Why does heart rate rise at the same pace?

During prolonged exercise, heart rate can rise while blood pumped per beat decreases (cardiovascular drift), especially in heat. Ease off if the effort rises; take breaks, choose a cooler setting and hydrate appropriately. This alone is not proof of poor fitness. Wingo et al., cardiovascular drift; heat stress review. Explore the science: Zones, thresholds and VO₂ max

Three-zone research models and five-zone watches use different boundaries. In many five-zone systems Zone 2 means easier aerobic work; research Zone 2 may not mean that. A watch estimates zones; some medicines, such as beta blockers, can change heart-rate response. AHA medication and exercise guidance. Do not translate a BFit effort number into a watch zone. A threshold describes a boundary in the body's response to workload. Researchers distinguish exercise-intensity domains based on physiological responses. Their boundaries depend on the method used and should not be treated as interchangeable with watch zones. At very high workloads, exercise may end before oxygen uptake reaches its maximum. These are not our rating categories. ACSM/ESSA 2025 consensus.

VO₂ max is the maximum rate at which the body takes up and uses oxygen during intense exercise. It measures aerobic capacity; maximal aerobic speed and mechanical power at VO₂ max are different measurements. You can make progress without measuring it; no maximal test required. For a running-specific zone guide, use Run Strong.

Chapter 5

How do we build a conditioning workout?

What changes when I change the rest?

When you cycle for one challenging minute, two minutes of easy pedaling may help you repeat it. With less recovery, the next effort may be harder. I choose work duration, effort, recovery, repetitions and activity based on what the workout is meant to improve. Buchheit and Laursen, interval programming.

Start with steady activity (continuous work) or controlled intervals (work alternating with recovery). A workout may use either. More specialized methods require preparation.

How much time will your workout take?

Four one-minute efforts with two minutes easy after each = four minutes work, eight minutes recovery, 12 minutes main block. With five minutes warm-up and five cooldown, 22 minutes total. Count recovery after the last interval too. Work-to-rest ratio is one effort's duration compared with its recovery (1 minute:2 minutes = 1:2).

Explore the science: HIIT, tempo and anaerobic capacity

HIIT is a subset of intervals using relatively high-intensity work; introductory controlled intervals are not necessarily HIIT. Sprint interval training involves very intense, often all-out/supramaximal efforts; repeated-sprint training typically uses brief maximal sprints with limited recovery. Neither is prescribed here. Repeated-sprint review; interval methods review. Anaerobic capacity refers to the amount of energy that can be supplied through nonoxidative pathways during demanding work; it is distinct from aerobic power, not a separate automatic training path. Energy contribution measurement review. Tempo is a broad term for sustained purposeful effort; threshold work targets a physiological boundary we do not measure. Fartlek means varying pace, for example walking faster between landmarks.

Both continuous and interval training can improve aerobic fitness. A 2026 meta-analysis of 115 trials found a larger average VO₂ max improvement with HIIT, while several other outcomes were similar. This does not mean every reader should begin with HIIT. A separate 2026 review of 239 HIIT protocols found that work bouts of at least two minutes generally accumulated more time at high oxygen uptake than shorter bouts. This is a short-term response during exercise, not proof of greater long-term improvement or an optimal interval duration for every person. Long-term comparison; acute near-VO₂ max review.

What is MetCon?

MetCon is short for metabolic conditioning. Coaches often use the term for workouts that combine repeated movements, such as squats and marching. Your muscles may tire before your breathing becomes difficult. That is a different challenge from a demanding cycling interval. Resistance circuit review.

Example: chair squat, standing march, stable incline push-up and supported low step-up (or march). Use familiar movements with 30 seconds work, 30 seconds rest/transition, twice around: eight minutes main (four work and four rest). Effort 4–6, controlled technique. Rest or simplify if arms or legs tire first. Don't rush repetitions or keep going until you physically cannot do another one. Moderate circuits can be appropriate before advanced conditioning, after learning movements. Use a separate circuit progression.

Choose the method that helps you reach your goal and recover for the next workout. A demanding session does not automatically mean a better adaptation.

Chapter 6

Build your conditioning plan

Where should I start?

I start with what you can comfortably do now. Two people with different goals may need the same first workout.

  • Build Stamina — keep going longer.
  • Improve Work Capacity — recover between efforts.

You can change your goal. Neither requires all-out efforts.

Choose an activity

Choose something you can safely access and repeat: walking, marching at home, cycling, rowing, elliptical or stairs. Running is an option if you already tolerate it; see Your First 5K or Run Strong for running-specific training. Familiar circuits have their own progression.

Swimming can be an aerobic activity. Automated swimming workouts are not available in this release. If you choose to swim independently, use a familiar pool length and rest at the wall. Use a lifeguarded pool, swim with a buddy, never alone. If you cannot swim a length safely, choose supervised lessons or a land activity. This guide does not include breath-holding, underwater challenges or open-water swimming. Red Cross water safety; CDC drowning prevention.

Tell me what you already do

How long can you comfortably perform this particular activity? How often have you done it recently? Have you tried controlled intervals? How did you feel the next day? If you don't know, that's fine; we start conservatively. Your report of comfortable activity helps choose a starting point; harder progression depends on the guide’s recorded comparable workouts and recovery. No maximum-effort test required. Strength or fitness in another activity does not establish readiness in this one.

Tell me your available days, total visit time (including preparation and recovery), existing strength/cardio/sports and whether more demanding exercise is appropriate. Health screening is separate from fitness history. Follow professional advice and restrictions; the guide cannot provide clearance. See the safety guidance before starting.

How will this fit your week?

Make this my conditioning plan: two main sessions, with an optional easy third visit if recovered.

Fit it around my current training: count suitable activity you already do and avoid adding unnecessary work. If you use another BFit guide, tell this plan about the activity you've already done. The guides don't share records automatically.

One day? Start with easy or steady work. A prepared reader may alternate steady and controlled intervals across visits when the recorded readiness requirements are met. One weekly workout is still a useful starting point. You may progress more slowly than someone doing two.

You can stay at a level indefinitely. Moderate circuits may suit someone earlier if the movements are familiar. More demanding intervals have separate readiness requirements.

Example first visit

If you can comfortably cycle for five minutes, start with five easy minutes warming up, five easy minutes cycling and five easy minutes cooling down (15 minutes total). If five minutes without stopping is too much, try short periods of easy movement: for example, one minute of easy walking, a comfortable rest, another minute if ready, with a gradual start and finish. Stop earlier if needed. No need to force five minutes or an arbitrary rest duration. Your goal can remain improving work capacity while you build a base.

Your workouts

What should I do first?

Set up your equipment safely and begin at an easy pace. Follow your plan's work and recovery periods; ease down at the end if appropriate. Record what you actually did. If something feels unusually wrong, stop rather than finishing a timer. See safety guidance.

Main block 6 minutes: 90 seconds work, 270 seconds recovery. Total 16 minutes. If breathing does not settle, extend recovery once if appropriate (a conservative guide rule, not a physiological limit); if still not ready, end the demanding portion and cool down if safe. Do not continue through warning symptoms.

Only have 20 minutes?

A four-interval session (one minute work, two minutes easy) takes 22 minutes with preparation and cooldown. Three intervals take 19 minutes. Choose a time-adjusted version or an easier alternative. Do not remove recovery to squeeze in another interval. Short on time is different from unable to finish because of fatigue.

What should I record?

Record actual work, effort, movement quality, extra rest and why you stopped early if applicable. Optional output: distance, watts or pedal revolutions per minute (rpm) with the same equipment/settings. A timer measures time, not whether you physically exercised. Confirm actual exercise; leave unknown measurements blank. Manual records are self-reported.

Check recovery the following day: normal for you, more tired than usual, or fatigue limiting ordinary activity. Use the safety route for pain or concerning symptoms. Do not record next-day recovery before the next day.

Swimming is not prescribed in this release. If you swim independently, record it as outside activity after leaving the water. Never interact with a phone in the pool.

If you need to stop or miss a visit

Pause when necessary, finish early or skip if you did not start. Record only what happened. Resume training without doubling demanding sessions. Phone browsers may suspend timers when locked/backgrounded; confirm uncertain time rather than automatically counting it. Use manual recording if a phone is unsafe or impractical.

Chapter 8

Combine conditioning with your other training

Do I need to add more cardio?

Maybe not. Games, classes and existing cardio contribute to your workload. A hard game is not the same as a controlled interval test. Count activity once and do not assume every minute qualifies as moderate/vigorous aerobic exercise. Decide which quality matters most right now.

Current trainingHow I would fit conditioning

Aerobic and strength training can generally coexist. A 2022 meta-analysis found no average reduction in muscle growth or maximal strength compared with strength training alone, although explosive-strength improvements were more susceptible to interference. If priorities share a day, do the priority quality work while fresh, separate demanding sessions where practical, and adjust if recovery suffers. No guarantee of zero interference and no universal mandatory rest-hours rule. Concurrent training meta-analysis.

If the week changes

Change future days without rewriting history. Back-to-back easy sessions can be appropriate if recovered. Don't do two workouts to make up for a missed one. After more than a week away from the activity, repeat a suitable previous dose or go easier. A normal once-weekly routine is not automatically a missed week. After about two weeks away from that activity, reduce the return workload and avoid hard efforts initially. These are conservative coaching defaults, not exact detraining thresholds. After illness/injury, follow individual guidance. Do not override safety concerns to stay on schedule.

Why hasn't my workout changed?

I want you to repeat a workload before increasing it. Our default is two comparable successful sessions at the current prescription with controlled movement, intended effort and normal next-day recovery. If the prescription changes, the comparison starts again. This is a coaching rule, not a universal biological cutoff.

Prior training informs starting point but is not guide attendance. Record outside activity without inventing time.

If you didn't record a measurement, leave it blank. You can still review your completed workouts and how you felt. Choose whether to maintain, adjust or change your goal.

Chapter 10

Common questions

What your body is doing

Do I need to catch a second wind?

No. Warming up and finding a sustainable pace may help you feel more comfortable. Never push through concerning symptoms waiting for it. See Chapter 2.

Does lactate make me sore the next day?

Lactate is not the cause of delayed-onset muscle soreness. The burning feeling during exercise and soreness afterward are different; neither proves a workout was effective. Lactate and soreness review. See Chapter 3.

What does hitting the wall mean?

A major drop in sustainable performance during prolonged endurance exercise, commonly discussed in marathon running. Reduced carbohydrate availability, pacing, heat and other fatigue factors can contribute; this isn't ordinary tiredness after a few minutes. Unexpected severe symptoms require stopping and appropriate help. Marathon-specific review. See Chapter 2–3 and Run Strong for running-specific applications.

Does sweating more mean I got fitter?

Sweat helps cool your body as it evaporates. Heat, clothing and individual differences affect it. Do not deliberately overheat or restrict fluids to sweat more. Heat stress review.

Intensity and energy use

Is Zone 2 best for burning fat?

At easier intensities, fat often supplies a larger share of exercise energy. The amount used per minute is different and may peak at a moderate intensity. Long-term body-fat change depends on more than which fuel you use during one workout. Choose activity you can sustain and recover from. Fuel use review; Body composition comparison; umbrella review. See Chapter 4.

Is the afterburn effect real?

Yes: oxygen consumption can remain above resting levels after exercise, called excess post-exercise oxygen consumption (EPOC). The extra expenditure varies with the workout and the person; harder workouts can increase it. EPOC alone does not establish a meaningful long-term fat-loss advantage or justify maximizing fatigue. EPOC systematic review.

Do I need a heart-rate monitor?

No. Use effort, breathing and movement quality. A watch adds information but cannot override symptoms or determine all training demands. See Chapter 4.

Is HIIT better than steady activity?

Both can improve aerobic fitness; the right method depends on your goal and readiness. You don't need HIIT to progress. See Chapter 5.

Is MetCon the same as HIIT?

Not necessarily. A circuit may challenge your muscles before your breathing becomes very demanding. Choose the method based on what you want to improve. See Chapter 5.

Making conditioning fit your life

Will cardio ruin my muscle gains?

Aerobic and resistance training can generally coexist; explosive strength may be more sensitive to combined demanding sessions. Manage workload and priorities. Concurrent training meta-analysis. See Chapter 8.

Can I improve without running?

Yes. Walking, cycling, swimming and other suitable activities can develop aerobic fitness. For running performance, running-specific practice matters. See Chapter 6.

Why am I fit at cycling but struggle with another activity?

Some adaptations transfer, but movements, muscle demands, coordination and impact differ. Establish a new baseline. Running/cycling cross-training review. See Chapter 1 and 6.

I already play sports or attend classes. Do I need another interval day?

Not necessarily. Count existing demanding work; easy activity or rest may be more useful. See Chapter 8.

Why hasn't my plan increased?

Repeating a useful workout helps establish readiness. Missing recovery, changed activities or excessive fatigue can delay progression. See Chapter 9.

What if I'm too tired to finish?

Slow down, recover or stop the demanding work; record what happened. Don't force a timer or double up. New pain or concerning symptoms require stopping and following safety guidance. See Chapter 3 and 7.

Can I change my goal or activity?

Yes. Keep your past records and establish a suitable baseline for the new activity. See Chapter 6.
