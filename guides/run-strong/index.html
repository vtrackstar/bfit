Before you start · when to back off

Choose a familiar route, account for weather, and follow any exercise restrictions your clinician has given you. Stop for sharp or worsening pain, pain that changes your stride, or feeling acutely unwell. Seek urgent medical help for chest pain, fainting, confusion, or severe unusual breathlessness. Persistent or recurring pain deserves assessment. Injury rehabilitation needs individual guidance.

02

Effort first. Pace with context.

The same speed can ask more of you on a different day.

Pace tells you how fast you are moving. Effort tells you how demanding it feels. Hills, heat, wind, and fatigue can change their relationship. RPE means rating of perceived exertion: 0 is rest, 10 is maximal effort.

EffortRPE, roughlyA useful cue
Easy2–4 / 10Comfortable conversation; breathing under control
Steady4–5 / 10Purposeful running; conversation takes more effort
Controlled hard6–7 / 10Short phrases; focused and repeatable
Hard8–9 / 10Only a few words; limited-duration work

These ranges overlap because the boundaries are approximate. A short fast stride may feel mechanically demanding before breathing or heart rate catches up.

For easy runs, use comfortable conversation as your starting cue. Slow down or take a walking break when needed. Research supports speech comfort as a practical intensity cue, but it does not calculate an exact threshold pace. [1]

How to get a useful pace target

How to read running numbers

m means metres; km means kilometres. A 5K is 5 km (about 3.1 miles); a 10K is 10 km (about 6.2 miles). One lap in lane 1 of a standard outdoor track is 400m. Four laps are 1600m; a mile is about 1609m.

5:00/km means five minutes per kilometre; 8:00/mile means eight minutes per mile. A smaller time per distance is faster. 4 × 2 minutes means four repetitions lasting two minutes each. 2 × (3 × 200m) means two sets of three 200m repetitions. See how recovery fits between them.

  1. New or returning to running? Start with easy effort and time to plan your first workouts.
  2. Have a recent race? Divide time by distance for average race pace. A 25-minute 5K is 5:00/km, approximately 8:03/mile, or 2:00 per 400m.
  3. Read the prescription. Current 5K pace can inform a 5K-paced session; it is not your easy pace or a universal interval pace. Use effort to check that the target fits the day.
Worked example · one runner, different pace decisions

Sam recently ran a 25:00 5K on a flat course and already handles regular easy runs and controlled intervals.

  • Easy day: Sam slows until full sentences feel comfortable, then records the pace. The race result does not set an easy-run speed.
  • Current-5K-pace practice: 4 × 400m at about 2:00 each, with 90 seconds easy walk/jog between, after 10 easy minutes; finish with 5 easy minutes. That is 1,600m of work and about 27½ minutes including recoveries. This practices repeatable race pace.
  • Threshold day instead: use the 2 × 6-minute example, choosing controlled hard effort with short phrases and consistent execution. Sam should expect a slower pace than the recent 5K race pace; the exact difference is learned from response, not a fixed subtraction.
  • Hot, windy or hilly day: accept slower splits to preserve the purpose. If the first rep is already a strain, adjust now instead of chasing the original number.

Choose one demanding session for the week here. Record both pace and effort so the next decision has context.

A recent performance in comparable conditions is more useful than a distant personal best. An established runner may use a time trial, but it replaces another demanding session. A goal time helps plan the destination; today's repetition length, pace, recovery, and volume must fit current readiness.

Heart rate and splits, in plain language

Heart rate helps reveal patterns, but estimated maximums and watch zones can be inaccurate for an individual. Short efforts also finish before heart rate fully responds. Read it alongside effort and conditions.

Split: time for one segment. Even splits: similar segment times. Negative splits: the later portion is faster. Positive splits: the later portion is slower. Lap pace describes one segment; average pace may include easy recoveries.

Know what you’re running.

Easy describes effort. Long describes relative duration. Fartlek and intervals describe structure. These labels overlap: a threshold session can use intervals, and a long run can include a progression.

Use it when you want practice sustaining running for longer. Extra duration counts as demand even at easy effort.

For someone recently handling 25-, 30-, and 40-minute runs, the 40-minute run is the long run.

Steady runsSustained running above easy, below threshold-style effort.+

Use it when you want a controlled step up in sustained effort without making the session very hard.

EXAMPLE

For a runner comfortable with 30 minutes: 10 easy + 10 steady + 10 easy.

Adjust it Keep the middle segment easy if the opening running feels unusually difficult.

Where it fits As a planned moderate session. Count it when deciding how much harder work fits.

Optional library entries (new tab): Steady Run

Tempo, threshold & cruise intervalsAn effort target and two ways to organize sustained work.+

Use it when you want to practice sustaining controlled hard running. Tempo is a broad coaching label: ask what effort is intended. Threshold describes the intended intensity near an upper sustainable aerobic boundary; here the practical cue is controlled hard, with short phrases possible. Cruise intervals divide threshold-style work into bouts with short easy recoveries.

EXAMPLE

For a regular runner used to 30–40-minute runs and controlled hard work, choose one: continuous — 10 easy + 12 controlled hard + 8 easy = 30 minutes; broken — 10 easy + 2 × 6 controlled hard, with 2 easy between + 8 easy = 32 minutes. Both contain 12 minutes of work; the break changes how it is sustained.

Adjust it Start with the broken version if uninterrupted work is harder to control. Shorten a bout or lower the effort if the first is already a struggle. These effort cues do not measure a laboratory threshold.

Where it fits One demanding session with easier training around it. Replace another workout rather than adding it on top.

Optional library entries (new tab): Tempo Run · Cruise Intervals

Progression / cutdown runsThe main run gets faster through planned stages.+

Use it when you want to practice changing gears while keeping control of your pacing.

EXAMPLE

For a regular runner with experience of faster work: 10 minutes easy → 8 steady → 4 controlled hard → 5 easy cooldown. Total: 27 minutes.

Adjust it Keep the final stage steady, or stay easy throughout, if effort rises too quickly. “Cutdown” can also describe successively faster repetitions.

Where it fits Treat a hard-finishing progression as a demanding session. Do not attach one to every easy run.

Optional library entries (new tab): Progression Run

Fartlek & surgesFaster and easier running within one run.+

Use it when you want to practice pace changes without relying on measured track distances. Fartlek means speed play; a surge is a deliberate increase in pace.

EXAMPLE

For a regular runner accustomed to some faster work: 10 easy minutes + 6 × (1 minute brisk / 2 minutes easy) + 5 easy minutes. Total: 33 minutes; brisk work: 6 minutes. Brisk is controlled and repeatable.

Adjust it Use fewer cycles or gentler surges. Keep the easy sections easy.

Where it fits Its place depends on the effort. This example occupies the week’s faster-work slot.

Optional library entries (new tab): Fartlek Run

Intervals, repetitions & laddersWork bouts separated by stated recoveries.+

Use it when you want a specific amount of work at an effort you would not hold continuously for the whole session. A repetition is one work bout; some coaching systems use the word more narrowly.

EXAMPLE

A regular runner familiar with intervals: 10 easy minutes, 4 × 2 minutes at controlled hard effort with 2 minutes easy between reps, then 6 easy minutes. Total: 30 minutes; work: 8 minutes.

Adjust it Reduce repetitions or pace if execution breaks down. A ladder changes bout length, such as 1–2–3–2–1 minutes; its purpose still depends on effort and recovery.

Where it fits A designated workout slot. Read every variable; “intervals” alone does not tell you how hard the day is.

Optional library entries (new tab): Running Intervals · Running Ladder

HIIT · high-intensity interval trainingHard, repeatable efforts with easier recovery between them.+

What it means: HIIT is a broad label for repeated demanding efforts separated by recovery. The exact effort, repetition length, rest, and number of bouts matter. Not every interval is HIIT: an easy run–walk session and controlled threshold intervals serve different purposes.

EXAMPLE

For an established runner who already tolerates harder sessions, the guide's 3 × 3-minute hard-but-repeatable example, with 2 easy minutes between, is one HIIT-style aerobic workout. It includes a warm-up and cooldown. It is not a beginner starting session or a test of maximal effort.

Adjust it: Keep the repetitions controlled enough to repeat. If pace or mechanics break down, reduce the demand or finish easily. Count the workout as a hard day, and make surrounding running easier.

Where it fits: A planned quality session when current running experience and weekly recovery support it. Use it instead of another hard workout, not on top of one.

Strides & accelerationsShort, smooth faster efforts with a gradual build.+

Use it when you want small doses of faster running while staying coordinated and relaxed. Acceleration describes the increase in speed.

EXAMPLE

After a usual easy run, a runner familiar with faster efforts adds 4 × 15 seconds. Build speed gradually, run quickly while relaxed, then ease down; do not sprint all-out. Walk 60–90 seconds between efforts, taking longer if breathing or control has not settled. Finish with 3 easy walking minutes.

Adjust it Use fewer efforts or less speed. Stop if coordination deteriorates. For a first introduction to pace changes, see the readiness example.

Where it fits After an easy run or within a warm-up. Full recovery protects the quality of these short efforts.

Optional library entries (new tab): Strides · Running Acceleration

Hill repeats & short hill sprintsThe slope, duration, effort, and recovery define the session.+

Use it when you want controlled uphill work. Longer repetitions sustain effort; brief hill sprints emphasize speed and need fuller recovery and prior preparation.

EXAMPLE

For a regular runner familiar with hills: 10 easy minutes, then 4 × 30 seconds uphill at controlled hard effort on a gentle, even slope. Walk down and allow at least 90 seconds between efforts, longer if needed to regain conversational breathing and control. Finish with 5 easy minutes. Uphill work totals 2 minutes; full-session time varies with recovery.

Adjust it Choose a slope you can run without straining; reduce duration or repetitions. Walk the descent under control.

Where it fits These hill repeats replace another demanding run. Short maximal hill sprints are a separate advanced choice.

Optional library entries (new tab): Hill Repeats · Short Hill Sprint

Ins and outs & broken distancesWays to divide faster running into smaller pieces.+

Use it when you want pace exposure with easier running or rest built into the structure.

EXAMPLE

Teaching example for an experienced track runner: use the track warm-up, then 4 continuous cycles of 100m at current 1600m race pace / 100m easy jog. The jog is the recovery; no extra rest between cycles. Finish with 8 easy minutes. Total block: 800m, including 400m at the faster pace. For a current 6:00 1600m runner, the fast 100m target is 22.5 seconds.

Adjust it Slow the fast segments or use fewer cycles if the easy segments stop feeling controlled. A broken distance divides a target distance into repetitions; the advanced case study explains broken 1600s.

Where it fits Within a planned faster session. Use the stated pace and recovery to practice controlled changes in speed.

Optional library entries (new tab): Ins and Outs · Broken-Distance Repetitions

Speed enduranceMaintaining fast running with the intended quality.+

Use it when an experienced runner needs repeated exposure to event-relevant speed. The term varies between coaching systems, so the numbers must explain it.

EXAMPLE

For a prepared middle-distance runner with recent 800m racing and fast-repetition experience: use the track warm-up, then 3 × 200m at current 800m pace with 3 minutes walking/easy recovery between repetitions, followed by 8–10 easy minutes. Work totals 600m. A current 2:40 800m averages 40 seconds per 200m.

Adjust it End the faster work when you can no longer hold the pace with control. If this speed is unfamiliar, use the first pace-change example instead.

Where it fits A demanding session that replaces other fast work and is separated from racing or another hard workout.

Optional depth · VO₂ max, threshold, energy systems, and economy

Aerobic endurance

Your ability to sustain predominantly aerobic running. Practically: how long you can hold the intended effort, repeat the work, and recover.

VO₂ max

The maximum rate at which you take up and use oxygen during intense exercise. It is a measure of aerobic fitness. [2]

Aerobic power vs capacity

Maximal aerobic power commonly refers to VO₂ max. Aerobic capacity is often used for the same thing; I use “aerobic endurance” when I mean sustained work ability.

VO₂ max workouts

A label for intervals intended to create a high aerobic demand. Pace, bout length, and recovery influence the response; the label does not establish that you reached VO₂ max. See the longer aerobic-interval example and effort comparison.

Lactate & threshold

Lactate participates in energy metabolism and can be used as fuel. Thresholds describe changes in the response to increasing intensity; tests and definitions differ, so effort cues are estimates. [3]

Aerobic & anaerobic

Both contribute during running. Aerobic energy production uses oxygen; anaerobic pathways do not directly require it in their reactions. A fast repetition does not switch the aerobic system off.

Running economy

The energy or oxygen cost of running at a given submaximal speed. A lower cost under comparable conditions means better economy.

Tempo, threshold or VO₂-focused intervals?

“Tempo” is used broadly by coaches. In this guide, the tempo card means threshold-oriented, controlled hard running: sustained work around the upper end of a manageable aerobic effort. Exact physiological thresholds depend on the test and definition; use the execution cues below to choose the session.

Choice / purposeWork and recovery in this guideWhat a good finish looks like
Continuous tempo / threshold · sustain effort12 controlled hard minutes, without a breakFocused but controlled; no desperate kick to finish.
Cruise intervals · accumulate similar effort in pieces2 × 6 minutes controlled hard, 2 easy minutes betweenSecond bout resembles the first; the break helps preserve control.
VO₂-focused intervals · higher aerobic demand3 × 3 minutes hard but repeatable, 2 easy minutes betweenBreathing is much harder, only a few words; pace and mechanics remain repeatable.

Shorter recovery does not automatically improve a workout. Threshold work asks for sustained control; harder aerobic intervals need enough recovery to repeat their intended demand. Select an option that fits your preparation.

Applied example · longer aerobic intervals

For an established runner who has already completed controlled faster sessions: 10 minutes easy + 3 × 3 minutes hard but repeatable, 2 minutes easy between + 8 minutes easy = 31 minutes total, 9 minutes hard work. Build into the first repetition; avoid an all-out start. Use a recent interval session to inform pace, then judge breathing, control and repeatability. Do not chase a watch zone during the opening seconds.

Replace another hard session, with easy/rest days around it. If the second bout requires straining or pace falls sharply, finish the quality work and review the starting pace and recovery. A later progression could add one bout after repeated successful execution; keep pace and recovery unchanged for that decision.

Research has shown improved VO₂ max after programs using aerobic intervals, including 4-minute bouts. The study used a different workout; this 3-minute version is my example. [13] The 200m comparison serves a different purpose: practicing event speed with selected recoveries.

When you want to try faster running

First look for a repeatable easy-running routine: your usual sessions feel manageable, you recover between them, and no pain is changing your stride. Also check the week for hard classes, leg training or sport. Use those cues to decide whether the week has room for a faster session. If easy running is still difficult, keep building that routine.

One introduction: for someone repeatedly comfortable with 20-minute easy runs but new to pace changes, replace one usual run with 10 easy minutes + 4 × (20 seconds a little quicker / 100 seconds easy) + 2 easy minutes = 20 minutes. The quicker parts total 80 seconds. Increase pace smoothly, stay relaxed and avoid sprinting. Keep other runs easy.

Repeat this session on a later week if control and recovery were good. Reduce to two quicker bouts, or keep the whole run easy, if the change felt too demanding. End the quicker work if you begin straining; stop running for pain that changes your stride. Adding speed is enough of a change—keep total time steady.

Build a complete faster session

How to tell whether a faster session fits

Do not choose 400m repeats, threshold intervals, or hard hills just because they are listed here. First establish that you can consistently complete your ordinary easy runs and recover from them. The example 4 × 400m session is for practicing a manageable pace—not a universal minimum workout or a complete 5K plan. The 2 × 6-minute threshold example includes 2 easy minutes between efforts; it should feel controlled and repeatable, not like two races. Add faster work by replacing an existing demanding session, not by adding another hard day.

  1. Set the purpose. Here: practice controlled changes in pace for a runner already comfortable with 30-minute runs and some faster work.
  2. Prepare. Begin with 10 minutes easy. For track-speed work, add familiar mobility, drills, and gradual accelerations as needed; a longer warm-up may be appropriate. Start the main set feeling ready, not already tired.
  3. Choose the main work. 6 × 1 minute brisk with 2 minutes easy after each. Pick an effort you can reproduce through the final repetition.
  4. Finish. Run or walk easily for 5 minutes. Use the cooldown to finish gradually and notice how you feel after the work.
  5. Set the adjustment. If control is slipping by the fourth bout, stop the brisk work and finish easily. Review pace, conditions, and recovery before the next attempt.

Total: 10 + 18 + 5 = 33 minutes. Only six minutes are brisk. If extra preparation is needed, include it in the full-session time.

Decode reps, sets, recovery, and total work

2 × (3 × 2 minutes) means two sets of three repetitions: six bouts and 12 minutes of work. With one minute easy between reps and three minutes between sets, the set break replaces the repetition break at that point.

Active recovery: walking or jogging. Passive recovery: resting without movement. A 1:1 work-to-rest ratio means equal time, not equal distance. Total work counts the work bouts; session volume includes warm-up, moving recoveries, and cooldown. Review distance and pace notation.

A track warm-up for runners already doing faster work

Start with 10–15 easy minutes. Then do 3 × 15-second relaxed strides, each with a gradual build and slowdown; walk 60–90 seconds between them. Walk or jog easily for another 2 minutes before the main set. Extend preparation if you need more time to feel loose and coordinated; skip the faster work if it still feels strained. Optional drills belong here only if you already know them. This sequence is for the experienced track examples, not a required warm-up for the beginner run–walk session.

Same distance, different workout: two ways to use 200m

For an experienced track runner with recent performances and practice at both speeds, choose one:

  • 6 × 200m at current 1600m pace, 90 seconds walk/jog between: 1,200m of work, emphasizing repeatable pace and controlled execution.
  • 3 × 200m at current 800m pace, 3 minutes walk/jog between: 600m of work, emphasizing faster event-paced running with more recovery.

Use the track warm-up and finish with 8–10 easy minutes. The faster version has less work and longer rests. Neither distance alone nor total metres tells you the purpose. A coach also considers what the runner can currently execute. Choose the option that fits your event and current preparation.

Read all four variables together.

Pace determines how fast; repetition length determines how long you sustain it; recovery affects readiness for the next bout; repetitions determine accumulated work. Four 3-minute efforts and twelve 1-minute efforts contain the same work time but can create different sessions.

05

Make running fit your week.

Count running, lifting, classes, and sports together when you plan your week.

Start with recent completed weeks, including the longest run, strength work, classes, and sports. Record minutes or distance consistently. Frequency means sessions per week; volume is the amount completed; intensity is how demanding the work is.

Build the strength session before adding holds

Organize strength work around four jobs: knee-dominant work such as a squat or split squat; hip-dominant work such as a hinge; calf work through a controlled raising and lowering action; and trunk work that you can control while breathing. Choose familiar movements that fit your equipment and current ability.

In Week B, Monday and Thursday are the planned strength sessions. Use your existing program or choose a complete option in the Foundation Training Guide (new tab). Keep its sets, repetitions and effort targets. The Exercise & Movement Library (new tab) supplies movement instructions and alternatives.

The calf holds below occupy an accessory slot within that structure. Keep dynamic strength work in the plan; replace an accessory when introducing a hold rather than increasing every part of the session at once.

Isometrics · controlled strength holds for runners

An isometric is a strength exercise where you produce force while holding a position—for example, keeping your heels raised instead of repeatedly lifting and lowering them. I use holds to develop strength in selected positions and provide controlled loading for muscles and tendons. Resistance, joint position, effort and hold duration determine the demand.

Progressive isometric training can develop muscle strength and change tendon mechanical properties. In a small 12-week study, loaded knee-extension holds increased knee-extension strength and tendon stiffness—the tendon structures resisted elongation more under load. That study tested a specific loading program. Here, holds support—not replace—dynamic strength work and gradually developed running. [12]

A modest introduction within strength work

Choose a position you can hold comfortably and steadily. Start with 2 × 20 seconds, resting 45–60 seconds between holds, at about 5–6/10 effort. Use both legs together initially. Keep breathing, hold a steady position and finish before fatigue makes you lose control. Adjust the effort to fit your strength and the rest of your week.

  • Monday in Week B: Supported Two-Leg Straight-Knee Calf Isometric Hold (library, new tab). On level ground, hold stable support, keep knees softly straight, raise both heels to a comfortable height and hold without bouncing.
  • Thursday in Week B: Supported Bent-Knee Calf Isometric Hold (library, new tab). Keep both knees comfortably bent while holding both heels raised; maintain the knee angle and lower under control.
  • Optional knee-focused alternative: Wall Sit (library, new tab), 2 × 20 seconds at a controllable depth with feet flat and back supported. Use a comfortable depth you can hold steadily. Replace another accessory rather than stacking all three holds.

Placement: use the accessory portion of a planned strength session. In Week C, a familiar hold can fit the existing light-strength option if recovery from Wednesday is good; otherwise keep Thursday restful. For middle-distance training, keep demanding holds in planned strength work and avoid introducing fatiguing holds immediately before fast repetitions. The first run–walk week does not require them.

Progress or adjust: repeat while learning the position. If execution and next-day comfort remain consistent, change one variable: for example, 20 to 25 seconds, a little more resistance, or a more demanding variation. Longer holds are not always the goal. If running or next-day comfort worsens, reduce or omit the holds and review the whole training load. Stop for new or increasing pain; persistent tendon pain needs individual assessment. Count hold work alongside your other strength training.

When the week gets crowded

Choose the priority session, preserve enough recovery to do it well, then reduce optional work. First consider extra conditioning finishers, duplicate hard sessions, or accessory volume. If recovery is poor, the priority session also changes. Do not cram missed workouts into the remaining days.

Cross-training means using another activity, such as cycling or swimming. It can provide useful exercise with different demands, but it is not a fixed mileage equivalent. Rest can simply be rest.

06

Let the goal guide the plan.

Use your completed sessions and recovery to decide what comes next.

Base training and specific preparation

Base training establishes repeatable running and the foundation for later demands. It can include familiar strength work and short faster efforts. Specific preparation makes selected sessions more relevant to the event while retaining supporting training.

Four weeks of decisions, not automatic increases

After Week 4: what next?

Use these four weeks to practice repeatable run-walk sessions. Running continuously can be a later goal. If both sessions felt comfortable and your next-day recovery was normal, repeat Week 4 once more or slightly lengthen the running portion of one session while keeping the total session time and number of days the same. If you struggled, return to a run/walk split you handled well. If you missed sessions, rebuild consistency before progressing.

Useful milestones: finish two repeatable easy sessions; maintain an easy conversational effort; recover comfortably the following day; then gradually build longer easy-running portions. Running continuously for 10, 20, or 30 minutes can be a later milestone, but it is not a deadline. A runner who already handles 10 continuous easy minutes should use the separate experienced-starting example rather than this 30-second interval on-ramp.

For the starting runner in Section 4. Keep the two sessions on nonconsecutive days and other training stable enough to judge the response.

Choose the change that serves the goal

  • Longer running bouts: practice sustaining easy running. In the example above, some walking time becomes running time; that raises the running demand even though session time stays constant.
  • Another repetition: accumulate more running while keeping the same breaks. A seventh 30/90 round adds 2 session minutes and 30 running seconds.
  • Another running day: spread practice across the week. First consider redistributing existing running into shorter sessions; adding a full extra session also increases weekly work and changes recovery spacing.

I compare planned work with recent completed work, including the longest run in the previous 30 days. That was the comparison used in a 2025 cohort: runs more than 10% longer than that recent longest run were associated with higher overuse-injury rates. It was observational, so the threshold is a caution flag, not a proven personal safe limit. For example, if your recent longest run was 5 km, a proposed 7 km is a large individual-session jump even if the weekly total barely changes. [7]

Use that comparison alongside effort, terrain, recovery and training history. Choose a smaller extension, repeat the current run, or redistribute the week when the jump is large. A separate novice-runner trial did not find fewer injuries with a program based on the weekly 10% rule. [6]

Both aerobic and anaerobic energy production contribute to middle-distance racing. In a study simulating track events, the aerobic share was greater for 1500m than 800m. That helps explain differences in emphasis; energy percentages are not a formula for dividing training time. [11] The choices above are my coaching examples.

ANNOTATED EXAMPLE · PREPARED MIDDLE-DISTANCE RUNNER

Practice mile pace without racing a mile.

For a runner with an established routine, recent mile/1600m performance, and experience of controlled track repetitions. The goal is consistent pace and relaxed execution.

  1. Prepare: the track warm-up: 10–15 easy minutes, 3 relaxed 15-second strides with 60–90 seconds walking between, then 2 easy minutes.
  2. Main set: 2 × (3 × 200m) at current mile/1600m race pace. Walk/jog 90 seconds between reps and 3 minutes between sets, replacing the shorter recovery there.
  3. Finish: 8–10 easy minutes. Work distance is 1,200m; full-session time depends on pace and preparation.

Purpose: learn consistent pace and relaxed execution. For a current 6:00 1600m runner, 200m averages 45 seconds.

Placement: replace another faster session, follow an easy/rest day, and keep the following day easy/rest. Avoid putting it immediately beside a race.

Adjustment: stop the fast work if the intended pace requires straining or deteriorating mechanics. Review pace, total work, and recovery; do not sprint the final rep to rescue the average.

How the earlier work connects to the 400s

First, accumulate repeatable work: the 800m session grows by one repetition each week. Next, practice changing pace: the surge ladders alternate faster and slower running inside longer repetitions. Then, accumulate longer work across several effort levels: the 1000m sets develop a different demand from the later 1600m-paced sessions. Finally, increase work at the intended event pace: week 10 contains twelve 400m repetitions (4,800m of work); week 11 adds one set for sixteen repetitions (6,400m). Pace and recovery stay the same.

The emphasis changes as the season progresses. Pace and recovery help explain the different demands of the 12,000m and 6,400m sessions. Judge each workout alongside the other running and speed work in that week.

Goal pace, execution and adjustment

I worked backward from postseason time goals, then adjusted to execution. These were the planned targets. I adjusted them based on each athlete’s training response. The 37/47-second surge targets belonged to the athletes I was programming for. A 37-second 200m corresponds to 4:56 pace for 1600m. For a runner targeting that time, the faster segments provide short exposures to goal pace, with slower running between them.

When an athlete could not finish, I stopped them and adjusted later prescriptions, often pace. I also consider total work, recovery, conditions and surrounding training. I used the broken-1600m workout for mile conditioning and to assess how well an athlete could repeat the target pace.

Takeaway: build the demands that lead into an event-specific workout, then adjust the next step to the athlete’s response. The shorter annotated mile session shows a smaller starting session. Build your progression around your preparation and recent training.

XC application · rhythm on grass and rolling ground

For a school, collegiate or adult XC runner already comfortable with 30-minute runs and controlled faster work: use a familiar, even-footed grass loop with gentle changes in gradient. Check the route before starting.

Session: 10 minutes easy + 4 × 2 minutes controlled hard with 2 minutes easy between + 6 minutes easy = 30 minutes total, 8 minutes of work. Keep a repeatable effort uphill, settle into rhythm over the crest, and shorten the stride as needed for footing. Accept changing pace across the terrain; use the clock and effort rather than forcing track splits.

Placement: replace one planned quality session, with easy/rest days around it. For a team athlete, the coach decides whether it belongs in that week. It should not be an extra session beside team intervals or a meet.

Adjust: move to firmer, flatter ground if slipping or steep descents prevent control. Reduce to three bouts if quality fades; that version totals 26 minutes with 6 minutes of work. A race week may call for a lighter session instead. Record terrain and conditions so comparisons between weeks are meaningful.

School and XC athletes

Discuss changes with your team coach. A meet is a demanding session; extra intervals should not be added on top of a full team week. For example, in a Saturday-meet week, ask whether a planned Friday workout should become easy running and familiar short strides. The answer depends on the coach's season priorities. School grade alone does not set mileage.

Persistent fatigue, recurring injuries, unintended weight changes, or menstrual changes deserve attention with a parent/trusted adult and an appropriate clinician or nutrition professional. Youth-running guidance emphasizes individual preparation, recovery, and adequate fueling. [8]

Taper example · arriving ready for a 5K

A taper reduces load before an important race. Keep familiar touches of pace while reducing accumulated work; avoid last-minute fitness tests. In a small trial of trained 1500m runners, the final interval session during a taper was performed at race speed or 115% of race speed. The faster condition produced more variable performance changes. This comparison informs caution about intensifying late work; it did not test the 5K schedule below or compare retaining pace with removing it. [9]

When to stop, repeat, or change

If opening repetitions are too difficult, reconsider pace or readiness. If quality fades later, examine total work, recovery, and early pacing. Stop or shorten the work before forcing poor execution. One difficult day does not automatically mean lost fitness.

Miss one session? Usually continue after checking recovery rather than doubling up. After a longer interruption, resume below the old workload and reassess. With fever or systemic illness, defer hard training; chest symptoms or unusual breathlessness warrant medical advice. Pain that worsens or changes your stride calls for stopping and assessment when needed.

A cutback/recovery week reduces demand, either planned or prompted by response. A benchmark is a repeatable session for comparison; a time trial is a demanding performance test. Keep conditions comparable and interpret the trend, not one score.
