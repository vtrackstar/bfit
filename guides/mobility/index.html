Understand your body

Why do I feel tight?

Imagine a mother who isn't ready to let her child walk to the park alone. First the child walks outside, then down the street, and eventually practices the whole trip. Each step gives them a chance to build experience and demonstrate what they can handle.

Movement practice can work in a similar way. Repeated, manageable movement may help you become more comfortable and develop the strength and control needed for a position. Your nervous system can influence how restricted a movement feels, but joint structure, muscle and tendon properties, and other factors matter too. Not every restriction can or should be stretched away.

Can you get there by yourself?

Sit on a sturdy chair. Use your hands under your right thigh to lift your knee comfortably toward your chest. Lower it, then try lifting without your hands. Repeat on the left. The movement at your hip is called hip flexion.

Range you reach with help is assisted or passive range; movement you produce yourself is active range. A difference may reflect strength, coordination or leverage. Notice getting there, holding and returning rather than assuming you need more stretching.

Strength near the end of your movement

A dancer might be able to lift a leg high with their hands but struggle to hold it there independently. End-range strength means becoming stronger near the limits of the movement you can comfortably control. If you already have plenty of flexibility, you may need strength rather than more range. Hypermobility is not the same as being flexible; recurring instability, pain or dislocations warrant individualized professional guidance.

Your feet help you understand the ground

Think about walking barefoot on grass compared with a smooth floor. Sensory receptors in your feet tell your nervous system about touch and pressure. Your brain combines this with information from muscles and joints, vision and the inner ear. Knowing where your body is without looking is called proprioception.

Beside a sturdy support, shift your weight gently forward and back. Notice pressure under your heel and the bases of your big and little toes. You don't need equal pressure everywhere or a rigid arch. Shoes are fine; use protective footwear if you have reduced sensation or need it for safety.

How much mobility is enough?

A taekwondo athlete may need high kicks; a basketball player needs controlled lateral movement and the ability to manage force. Neither needs to copy the other's range. Your goal might simply be putting on your shoes comfortably. You can maintain the movement you need instead of chasing more.

What should practice feel like?

Comfortable stretching, ordinary stiffness and manageable muscular effort can be normal. Stop a movement that causes pain, pinching, numbness, tingling or unusual instability. Seek appropriate professional guidance for persistent, worsening or concerning symptoms. This is general fitness education, not injury rehabilitation or medical clearance.

How to practice

Dynamic and static stretching

Before running, lifting or playing sports, I recommend dynamic mobility: move through comfortable positions and gradually prepare for the activity. If you prefer static stretching beforehand, keep it brief before explosive efforts and follow it with dynamic and activity-specific movement. After training, static stretching is an option if you want to work on flexibility; it is not required for recovery.

Foam rolling with intention

Think about removing bubbles from a screen protector rather than applying lotion. Move deliberately, notice the pressure and revisit a spot briefly if useful. This describes your approach, not a claim that you are pushing knots out of tissue. Try 30–60 seconds of manageable pressure, then repeat a comfortable movement. Skip rolling if it doesn't help. Avoid painful pressure, injured areas and direct pressure on joints.

Jump Start Athletics foam rolling demonstration (external)

Learn to lower, hold and move back out

Think about sitting down in a chair. You could drop into it, or you could lower yourself slowly. In the second version, your thigh muscles help control your descent while they lengthen under tension. This is called an eccentric muscle action. Standing back up uses a concentric action as those muscles shorten to produce movement. Holding a comfortable position without moving is an isometric action.

These abilities work together. A dancer might need to hold a raised leg; someone walking downstairs needs to control their descent; an athlete moving sideways needs to absorb force and then push away. Eccentric strength practice can help develop strength through a useful range, and research also supports improvements in flexibility, especially in the lower body. It does not automatically prepare you for high-speed sport.

Try three familiar examples
  • Chair sit-down: use a sturdy chair and hand support as needed. Take about three seconds to sit, then stand normally.
  • Calf raise: hold a stable counter, rise onto both forefeet and lower your heels over three seconds.
  • Hip hinge: soften your knees, move hips backward slowly, then stand. If familiar with lifting, a manageable Romanian deadlift adds resistance.

Start with one set of 4–6 comfortable repetitions, twice weekly if it fits your existing strength routine. This is a coaching starting point, not a required dose. Allow recovery between challenging sessions; unfamiliar eccentric work can cause soreness. Do not add load, range and duration together. Stop painful or uncontrolled movements.

Slow lowering is one way to practice eccentric control when muscles resist a force while lengthening. Not every slow movement is eccentric strength training, and you do not need maximal loads or eccentric-only training. If your current lifting already trains the same motion adequately, keep it rather than adding another workout.

Lifting weights and flexibility

In a Romanian deadlift, your hips move backward as your hamstrings lengthen under resistance. Training through a suitable range can develop strength and may improve flexibility. This is different from actively lifting and holding a straight leg. Choose according to what you want to do.

Find a movement you want to improve

Try the comfortable version first. Compare sides when relevant. Notice whether reaching, holding or returning is difficult. These explorations help choose practice; they do not diagnose a joint or muscle problem.

Reach a high shelf — shoulders

Raise your right arm forward and overhead, then lower it slowly. Try the left. This is shoulder flexion; your shoulder blade naturally moves too. If it is difficult, stand at a sturdy table, rest your hands on it and gently move your hips backward. If you have enough range but want control, practice independent reaches and short holds.

For reaching a back pocket, move one hand behind your back only as far as comfortable. This combines shoulder rotation and other movements; do not pull your hand upward by force.

Turn to look behind you — upper back

Sit with feet on the floor, arms crossed. Turn your chest left and right with hips generally facing forward. This is thoracic rotation. To explore extension, gently lift your chest and straighten your upper back, then relax. Try six comfortable repetitions. Sitting upright for longer also requires endurance; there is no single perfect sitting position.

For a screen-heavy day, Gamer Fit offers broader routines and movement breaks. You can finish this guide without opening it.

Put on shoes or sit cross-legged — hips

For knee lifting, use the chair exercise above. For outward rotation, gently move your right ankle toward your left thigh if comfortable. For inward rotation, keep your right thigh pointing forward with knee bent, and move your right foot outward. For outward rotation in this same seated setup, move your right foot inward. Reverse directions on the left. Use a small comfortable movement; do not force the knee.

For moving your leg backward (hip extension), stand beside support and gently move one leg behind you without relying on a large lower-back arch. For moving sideways (hip abduction and adduction), try supported sideways leg lifts and weight shifts.

Step sideways or prepare for a side lunge

Stand with feet comfortably apart beside sturdy support. Bend your right knee slightly as you shift toward that leg; keep your left leg comfortably extended. Return to the middle and repeat left. More comfortable weight transfer, a steadier position or a smoother return all count. Later, a controlled lateral lunge can develop strength. This is not clearance for fast cutting or jumping.

Bend forward or lift a straight leg — hamstrings and hips

Lie on your back with one knee bent and foot planted. Gently lift the other leg with hands supporting the thigh, then compare with an independent lift. Do not chase a maximum stretch. For a hip hinge, stand a small step in front of a wall, soften your knees, move your hips backward toward the wall and stand again. Progress toward a controlled Romanian deadlift only as appropriate.

Walk downstairs or squat — ankles

Face a wall with hands supported. Move your front knee forward over your foot while keeping the heel down, then return. This is dorsiflexion. For plantarflexion, sit and point your foot away from your shin; a supported calf raise trains the same direction under body weight. Try each side. Pinching at the front of the ankle is not a cue to push farther.

While seated, gently turn the sole inward and outward without turning the whole thigh (inversion and eversion). Practice small controlled movements. For balance, hold a stable support and notice how your standing foot adjusts.

Look at another monitor — neck

While seated, turn your head comfortably to each side, look gently up and down, and tilt toward each shoulder. These are cervical rotation, flexion, extension and lateral flexion. Do not force the range or perform aggressive circles. Stop for dizziness, unusual headaches or radiating symptoms and seek appropriate guidance.

Use a controller, open a jar or support your hands — wrists and hands

Rest your forearm and gently bend your wrist forward and backward (flexion and extension). Turn your palm up and down (forearm supination and pronation). Open and close your hand, spread fingers and touch your thumb to each fingertip. For push-up preparation, comfortable supported weight shifts against a wall may develop loading tolerance rather than more passive range.

Get up from a chair — knees

While seated, straighten and bend one knee comfortably. For strength and coordination, stand up from a sturdy chair using support as needed, then sit slowly. The movement involves your hips and ankles too.

90/90: practice the parts you need

In a seated 90/90 position with your left leg in front, your left hip rotates outward and your right hip rotates inward. Switch the legs and the directions switch. You do not need exact right angles.

I can sit here once I use my hands to get into position

First notice whether getting in, staying there or getting out is the difficult part. If the front leg feels limited, try a supported figure-four on a chair or lying down. For the back leg, try the seated inward rotation described above. Support your hands or sit on a cushion in 90/90 if useful. Practice a small assisted entry and return before trying a transition between sides.

Progress can be sitting at the same angle more comfortably, using less hand assistance if appropriate, holding steadily or returning smoothly. You can keep using your hands indefinitely. Stop painful movements; do not press your knees down. If floor sitting is not accessible, use chair-based rotation instead.

Overhead squat: combine movements

Try a comfortable squat and an overhead reach separately. Then try a shallow squat with your arms reaching forward, partway upward and eventually overhead if comfortable. Start unloaded and use a sturdy support for the squat alone if needed. Combining these actions changes balance and coordination; difficulty does not identify a specific tight muscle. You do not need to squat deeper to improve.

Fit mobility into your week

Preparation helps you use today's abilities; development improves them over time. Use a few relevant dynamic movements before training, optional static stretching afterward, and dedicated strength or control work when it serves your goal. Challenging loaded mobility counts as strength training; allow recovery and avoid duplicating work you already do.

Recognize progress

Repeat a familiar movement under similar conditions. Notice comfort, assistance, steadiness, return control and strength. If your movement meets your goal, maintenance is a successful outcome. Four weeks of practice is an opportunity to learn, not a deadline to become flexible.

Research and coaching approach

These studies inform my coaching recommendations. They do not validate the BFit workout algorithm or establish that every exercise is suitable for everyone.

Warm-up and stretching

Longer static stretching immediately before maximal efforts can temporarily reduce performance. The overall warm-up matters. My preference is dynamic preparation before sport and optional static stretching afterward; stretching afterward is not required for recovery.

Behm et al. (2016), systematic review of stretching and performance

Strength and available range

Resistance training can improve range of motion. Choose exercises and ranges that serve your goal; greater range alone does not demonstrate active control.

Afonso et al. (2021), strength training versus stretching · Alizadeh et al. (2023), resistance training and range of motion

Eccentric strength and flexibility

Systematic reviews support improvements in flexibility with eccentric resistance exercise, particularly lower-limb passive range of motion in healthy participants. Evidence does not establish that slow lowering is superior for every goal, or that these gains automatically transfer to active control or injury prevention.

Eccentric exercise improves joint flexibility in adults (2022) · Effects of eccentric resistance training on lower-limb passive joint range of motion (2023) · Eccentric strength training, flexibility and strength (2022)

Foam rolling

Rolling can temporarily improve range, and longer-term practice can also improve range in some populations. The screen protector analogy describes technique, not a tissue mechanism. The suggested 30–60 seconds is a coaching starting point rather than a proven optimal dose.

Wilke et al. (2020), acute effects of foam rolling · Konrad et al. (2022), foam rolling training effects

Foot sensation and balance

Receptors in the sole of the foot provide feedback relevant to standing balance. This does not establish that foot-awareness practice corrects pain or movement limitations elsewhere.

Strzalkowski et al. (2018), cutaneous afferent innervation of the foot sole · The sensory role of the sole of the foot (2020), review

Hypermobility and control

Research on exercise and rehabilitation in Ehlers-Danlos syndrome suggests potential benefits, but the evidence is limited. Being flexible is not a diagnosis. People with symptomatic instability or recurrent dislocations need individualized professional guidance; this general guide is not a rehabilitation program.

Buryk-Iggers et al. (2022), systematic review

Practice duration, two-session progression rules and four-week themes are BFit coaching defaults, not validated medical thresholds.
