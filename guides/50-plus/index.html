01 / Getting started

Start with where you are today

No fitness test required. Choose what sounds most like you right now. You can change your answers later.

You can still take comfortable walks or move gently on the other days.

Start with a safety check. Recent or repeated falls, unexplained dizziness, and significant balance problems deserve a conversation with a healthcare professional. A physical therapist can help determine suitable balance exercises. Don't try unsupported balance or floor drills on your own.

Before you start: Seek urgent medical care for new chest pain, fainting, severe breathlessness, or other serious symptoms. If you have a condition affecting exercise or have been told to restrict activity, follow your clinician's guidance. For training, choose stable support, clear floor space, and comfortable footwear.

The recommendations below are general starting ideas, not a medical assessment or individualized rehabilitation plan.

03 / Find your movement

Choose what you'll look forward to

Walking, cycling, and swimming are easy places to begin exploring cardio. But dancing or another activity can be your main choice if that's what keeps you moving. Try something once before deciding whether you like it.

Cardio means activity that raises your breathing and heart rate. At a moderate pace, you should still be able to talk, though singing would be harder.

Other activities I want to try (up to three)

These are optional. You'll still have a weekly routine if you don't choose one.

What is each activity like?

Cardio · Weight-bearing

Walking

A practical way to build endurance with little equipment. Brisk walking can count toward moderate aerobic activity.

First time and watch-outs
Try five to ten comfortable minutes on a familiar, even route. Use a walking aid if prescribed. Add time gradually. Walking alone may not provide enough strength or balance practice for every goal.
Cardio · Low impact

Cycling

Builds heart and lung endurance with relatively low impact. A stationary or recumbent bike can be a more stable choice.

First time and watch-outs
Start with easy resistance and check seat height. Outdoor cycling requires confident bike handling and a safe environment. Cycling is not weight-bearing exercise for bone health.
Cardio · Water

Swimming

Builds endurance while water supports your weight. Water walking can be an alternative to lap swimming.

First time and watch-outs
Choose a supervised pool and a comfortable depth. If you aren't a confident swimmer, start with lessons or water walking. Swimming does not provide the weight-bearing stimulus of walking.
Cardio · Social

Zumba and aerobics

Often music-led classes that combine repeated steps and movement patterns. They can improve stamina and coordination.

First time and watch-outs
Look for beginner or low-impact classes. Start at your own speed and skip jumps or quick turns you can't comfortably control.
Sport · Social

Golf

Can add walking, coordination, rotational movement, and outdoor time. The physical workload depends on how you play.

First time and watch-outs
Try the range or a short session. Walking the course provides more aerobic activity than using a cart. Warm up before repeated swings.
Sport · Coordination

Pickleball

A racket game played on a smaller court than tennis. It includes social interaction, quick steps, and changing direction.

First time and watch-outs
Try a beginner lesson with a short session. Rapid stops and turns may be too demanding for someone with balance or joint concerns; practice footwork first.
Class · Varies by format

Group fitness

An instructor leads a class, which might include cardio, resistance exercises, or balance practice.

First time and watch-outs
Ask what the class involves before joining. A gentle class isn't necessarily a full strength workout; some classes are quite demanding.
Resistance · Control

Reformer Pilates

Exercises using a moving carriage and spring resistance. It may help with movement control, strength, and flexibility.

First time and watch-outs
Book a beginner introduction and tell the instructor about mobility limits. It can complement strength training, but may not fully replace progressive strength work for every person.
Balance · Mobility

Tai chi and gentle yoga

Typically slower movement, controlled positions, and attention to posture. Some formats support balance and flexibility.

First time and watch-outs
Choose a class that offers chair or supported modifications. Avoid painful positions and unsupported standing challenges if you are unsteady.

04 / Build everyday strength

A little strength and balance work goes a long way

Cardio helps your endurance. To practice lifting, carrying, standing up, and steady movement, add resistance exercises and balance work. Many adults benefit from training all the major muscles on at least two days each week.

150 minWeekly moderate cardio is a general adult goal, not a first-week requirement.
2 daysMuscle-strengthening work each week is recommended for adults.
BalanceEspecially important in guidance for adults 65 and older; practice safely.

Starting point: after a gentle warm-up, try one set of about six to ten controlled repetitions per exercise, with a manageable effort. Progress toward one or two sets as tolerated; rest when you need to. Stop or modify any movement that causes concerning pain, dizziness, or loss of control. If you already train, use a suitable level rather than starting over.

Balance practice

Start near a sturdy counter with room to move. Try comfortable weight shifts or slow marching while holding on. If you're steady, progress gradually to less hand support or small steps in different directions. Don't practice difficult balance drills alone when falling is a concern.

Mobility and recovery

Gentle shoulder circles, ankle movement, and comfortable hip motion help keep daily movement familiar. Easy days matter. Sleep, joint comfort, medication effects, and the previous day's activity can affect how ready you feel.

06 / Four-week discovery

Try things. Keep what you enjoy.

These four weeks are a way to explore, not a deadline for results. If a week feels too challenging, repeat it or scale it down.

Week 1

Get comfortable

Try your starting routine. Learn where you can move safely and what a comfortable pace feels like.

Week 2

Try something new

Explore one class, sport, or cardio option you're curious about. You can observe a class before joining.

Week 3

Build a little

If you've been recovering well, consider adding five minutes to one or two comfortable cardio sessions or a small amount of strength practice.

Week 4

Decide what stays

Keep activities you enjoy, review your everyday confidence, and build the next week around what worked.

What if you're already active? Use the builder as a weekly check. Look for gaps in balance or strength, not just more exercise. You can skip the discovery weeks entirely.
07 / Review and keep going

What feels different after trying your routine?

Progress can mean moving with more confidence, enjoying an activity, or finding a schedule you're happy to repeat. Track what matters to you.

What went well this week?

Moving forward: If a routine feels good, repeat it. If it's too much, reduce the time or difficulty. If it feels easy and recovery is going well, progress gradually. A change in confidence is useful feedback, but isn't a medical test or proof that fall risk has changed.

Sources and scope

Where the guidance comes from

This guide is educational. The activity recommendations draw on general physical activity guidance and research on exercise and fall prevention. Exercise can reduce fall risk, but it cannot guarantee that someone won't fall.

  1. CDC: Physical Activity Guidelines for Adults (including adults 50–64).
  2. CDC: Physical Activity Guidelines for Adults 65+ (including balance activity).
  3. National Institute on Aging: Getting and Staying Active as You Age.
  4. Cochrane review: Exercise for Preventing Falls in Community-Dwelling Older People.
  5. CDC: Preventing Falls and Hip Fractures.

Evidence about fall prevention focuses primarily on older adults, often 65 and above. Not everyone over 50 has the same risks or needs. Prior injuries, health conditions, medications, and ability can all affect exercise selection.
