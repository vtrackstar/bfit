WHY KEEP MOVING?

What would you like your body to keep doing for you?

Carrying your bags, keeping up on a walk, feeling steady on stairs, dancing, playing a sport, or just having more energy for your day. That's a good place to start. Your routine can include the movements you want to get better at.

Strength for daily tasks

Getting out of a chair, lifting a bag, and using the stairs.

Stamina for the day

Walking, swimming, cycling, or dancing without needing to rush.

Confidence in movement

Practicing balance and controlled steps with appropriate support.

What the research tells us

You can build strength and fitness later in life. Choose a manageable starting point and repeat it long enough to see how you respond.

Muscle: strength can improve

Muscle responds when you give it work it can manage, then time to recover. Resistance training can improve strength and physical function in older adults.

What did researchers study?

A 2024 systematic review and network meta-analysis combined 151 randomized trials in older adults. It compared resistance-training volumes and reported improvements in physical function and lower-body strength across training approaches. The amount of work that is appropriate still depends on the person.

Read the review (PubMed)

Bones: the type of movement matters

Walking and dancing load the legs. Resistance exercise also challenges muscles and bones. Swimming and cycling build endurance with little impact. Weight-bearing and resistance exercises add another kind of challenge for bone health.

What does the bone research show?

In the LIFTMOR randomized trial, carefully screened postmenopausal women with low bone mass completed eight months of supervised resistance and impact training. The intervention improved measures of bone density and physical function compared with a low-intensity program. Participants were screened and trained under supervision. If you have low bone density, ask your healthcare professional about suitable exercise options.

Read LIFTMOR (PubMed) · NIAMS bone-health guidance

Balance: practicing makes a difference

Steady movement is a skill you can practice. Balance work can include holding a counter while shifting weight or learning controlled steps. The right starting point depends on how steady you feel.

How strong is the evidence?

A Cochrane review found that exercise programs reduced falls among older adults living in the community. The review examined structured exercise programs over time. Recent falls or unexplained dizziness deserve medical attention to help identify possible causes and choose safe exercises.

Read the Cochrane review

Heart, mood, and company

Regular activity supports cardiovascular health. It can also help sleep, mood, and feeling connected to other people. A walk with a friend or a class you enjoy still counts as meaningful movement.

Where can I read more?

The CDC and National Institute on Aging describe benefits for heart health, mental well-being, function, and quality of life. Benefits and exercise needs differ from person to person.

CDC: Benefits for older adults · NIA: Health benefits

Start at your level. General guidelines encourage adults to work toward 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity weekly, plus muscle-strengthening on at least two days. Guidance for adults 65+ also emphasizes balance. Build toward those totals gradually, starting with what feels manageable. If you have osteoporosis, symptoms, recent falls, or unexplained dizziness, follow medical advice on safe exercise options.
Sources and scope

Where the guidance comes from

This guide is educational. The activity recommendations draw on general physical activity guidance and research on exercise and fall prevention. Exercise is one part of fall prevention, alongside attention to vision, medications, footwear, and home hazards.

  1. CDC: Physical Activity Guidelines for Adults (including adults 50–64).
  2. CDC: Physical Activity Guidelines for Adults 65+ (including balance activity).
  3. National Institute on Aging: Getting and Staying Active as You Age.
  4. Cochrane review: Exercise for Preventing Falls in Community-Dwelling Older People.
  5. CDC: Preventing Falls and Hip Fractures.
  6. NIAMS: Exercise for Your Bone Health.
  7. Arthritis Foundation: Working Out With Pain.
  8. 2024 review of 151 randomized resistance-training trials in older adults.
  9. LIFTMOR randomized trial: bone mineral density and physical function.
  10. CDC: Health benefits of activity for older adults.
  11. National Institute on Aging: Health benefits of exercise.

Evidence about fall prevention focuses primarily on older adults, often 65 and above. Your starting point matters. Prior injuries, health conditions, medications, and current ability all help determine which exercises suit you.
